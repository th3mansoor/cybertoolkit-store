How to Spot a Phishing Email: 10 Warning Signs

A practical guide for everyday internet users · 8-minute read

A convincing email can look as if it came from your bank, a delivery company, your employer, or a service you use every day. But its real purpose may be to steal your password, collect financial information, or trick you into installing malicious software.

This type of deception is called phishing. Attackers use misleading messages and websites to persuade people to reveal information or take unsafe actions. Learning to recognize common warning signs can help you avoid many of these scams.

Important rule: If an unexpected message asks you to sign in, pay, disclose a verification code, or open an attachment, pause and verify the request through a separate, trusted channel.

10 warning signs of a phishing email

1. The sender's address looks wrong

Check the full email address, not just the display name. A message claiming to come from a familiar company may use a misspelled domain, an unrelated address, or an extra word or character.

A familiar logo or display name is not proof that the message is genuine. Even a plausible address should be considered alongside the message's content and the action it requests.

2. The message creates urgency or fear

Phrases such as "Your account will be closed today" or "Immediate payment required" are designed to pressure you into acting before you think. Scammers often use deadlines, threats, or alarming claims.

Do not use the message's links to resolve the supposed emergency. Open the company's official app or type its known website address into your browser instead.

3. Links lead somewhere unexpected

A link's visible text can hide its real destination. On a computer, you can usually hover over a link to inspect its destination without opening it. On a phone, avoid tapping suspicious links just to check where they lead.

Watch for misspellings, unusual subdomains, misleading lookalike characters, and domains that do not match the organization. HTTPS alone does not mean a website is trustworthy.

4. It asks for passwords or verification codes

Treat requests for passwords, one-time verification codes, recovery codes, or authentication approvals with extreme caution. Criminals may use stolen credentials or real-time phishing pages to take over accounts.

Never share a one-time code with someone who contacts you unexpectedly. Verify the request using the service's official website, app, or published support channel.

5. The greeting or details are suspicious

Generic greetings, incorrect names, unexpected account details, and awkward wording can be warning signs. However, polished grammar and personalized details do not prove that an email is legitimate.

Attackers can copy genuine business messages and use information obtained from public profiles or previous data breaches.

6. It contains an unexpected attachment

Be cautious with unexpected invoices, delivery notices, documents, archives, and executable files. Attachments may contain malicious content or direct you to a fake sign-in page.

If you were not expecting the file, confirm with the sender through a separate channel before opening it. Do not enable macros or bypass security warnings just because a message tells you to.

7. It requests unusual payments or gift cards

Requests to pay through gift cards, cryptocurrency, unfamiliar payment services, or unusual bank-transfer instructions deserve extra scrutiny. Business email compromise scams may impersonate managers, suppliers, or executives.

Verify changes to bank details and unusual payment requests using a known phone number or an established internal approval process.

8. The message is unexpected or out of context

An unexpected password reset, shared document, parcel notification, invoice, or account alert may be bait. Ask yourself whether you initiated the action and whether the message fits your recent activity.

If you are unsure, visit the service directly rather than following the message's instructions.

9. It asks you to bypass normal procedures

Be wary of requests to keep a transaction secret, skip approval, install remote-access software, change payment details immediately, or ignore normal security checks.

Verify unusual instructions with the person or organization using contact information you already trust—not the contact details supplied in the suspicious message.

10. It seems too good—or too alarming—to be true

Unexpected prizes, refunds, job offers, exclusive deals, and extraordinary account warnings can all be used to manipulate you. Scammers exploit excitement and fear alike.

Be especially careful if you must provide personal information, pay a fee, or sign in before you can receive the supposed benefit.

What to do when you receive a suspicious email

  1. Do not click its links or open unexpected attachments.
  2. Do not reply with personal information or verification codes.
  3. Verify the claim using the official app, known website, or trusted contact details.
  4. Report the message through your email provider's phishing-reporting option, if available.
  5. Delete it after reporting, following your organization's security procedures if it is a work account.

What if you already clicked a phishing link?

Do not panic. The right response depends on what happened after you clicked. Opening a link, entering a password, downloading a file, and running a program carry different levels of risk.

If you only opened the link

  • Close the suspicious page and do not enter any information.
  • Check that your browser and operating system are up to date.
  • If a file downloaded, do not open it; follow your device's security guidance.

If you entered a password

  • Use the service's genuine website or app to change the password immediately.
  • If you reused that password elsewhere, change it on those accounts too.
  • Sign out other sessions where possible and review recovery details and account activity.
  • Enable multifactor authentication if available.

If you shared financial details or a verification code

  • Contact your bank or relevant provider using its official contact details.
  • Ask about blocking transactions, securing the account, or replacing compromised payment details.
  • Secure the affected account and review recent activity.

If you downloaded or ran a suspicious file

  • Stop interacting with the file or program.
  • Use your device's trusted security software and follow its remediation guidance.
  • If it is a work device, contact your IT or security team promptly.

How to reduce your phishing risk

  • Use multifactor authentication: It can help protect accounts if a password is stolen. Passkeys or phishing-resistant authentication provide stronger protection where supported.
  • Use unique passwords: A reputable password manager can help you avoid reusing credentials.
  • Keep software updated: Install security updates for your operating system, browser, and applications.
  • Maintain backups: Keep important files backed up and make sure you can restore them.
  • Verify unusual requests: Use a separate trusted channel, particularly for payments and account changes.

Quick phishing safety checklist

  • Check the sender's full address and the actual destination of links.
  • Pause when a message demands urgent action.
  • Never share passwords or verification codes in response to unexpected requests.
  • Verify payment changes and sensitive requests independently.
  • Do not open unexpected attachments or bypass security warnings.
  • Report suspicious messages and act quickly if you disclosed information.

Final thoughts

Phishing messages can be convincing, and no single warning sign catches every scam. The most reliable habit is to slow down and verify unexpected requests independently—especially when money, passwords, or sensitive information are involved.
